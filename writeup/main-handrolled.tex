    \large \textbf{Abstract}
    \end{center}

\normalsize

Tensor networks are a natural quantum-native architecture for image encoding, since their contraction structure can capture an image's spatial hierarchy, but the relative merits of the candidates have not been established empirically. We survey tree, matrix-product and MERA topologies across four data encodings, three ans\"{a}tze and three readout protocols, and select a coherent quantum tree tensor network trained in noiseless simulation.

We then examine which of its quantum properties carry weight. Unitarity costs nothing: at $18\%$ fewer parameters the quantum node beats its unconstrained classical counterpart on shape perception and matches it on spatial relations, without the dropout and residual connections the classical tree needs. Coherence between levels is load-bearing: passing intermediate states as qubits rather than as measured scalars is worth $+9.9$ points. Bond dimension, held to one qubit per bond by simulation memory, limits the readout. Gradient variance decays polynomially rather than exponentially, so there is no barren plateau; yet most initialisations still fail to train, indicating a landscape of poor local minima rather than a flat one. The tower nonetheless resolves a compositional binding task using its topology alone, with no explicit positional parameters.

Substituted for a frozen classical vision encoder, it yields a vision--language model that is quantum-inspired end to end and improves compositional accuracy on both attribution and relation, though it does not transfer to general-purpose caption data.

\chapter{Introduction}


Quantum machine learning models promise large improvements over classical ones in both parameter count and expressiveness by taking advantage of quantum effects like entanglement and superposition. These advantages however come at a cost; some arise due to the computational limitations of the NISQ era \cite{nisq}, while others are intrinsic to the quantum paradigm.

In this work, we set off to explore possible architectures for a quantum-inspired image encoder. The field of quantum-inspired machine learning \cite{quantum_inspired} mostly revolves around using tensor network architectures. This framework allows for both a classical and a quantum model to be developed in parallel, an approach we adopt in this work. We explore how classical features, like dropout \cite{dropout} and residual connections \cite{residuals}, can be applied to quantum circuits and whether or not they are needed. Moreover, we investigate how the selection of ansätze and encoding methods affects the expressivity of the model, its depth and parameter efficiency.

Beyond architectural features, we evaluate and contrast the resulting tensor network's performance with contemporary MLP models on various benchmarks across perception, relation, and composition tasks.

As a secondary objective, we explore how this architecture performs within a broader multimodal context. Contemporary Vision Language Models (VLMs) heavily rely on contrastive learning architectures\cite{clip} that frequently exhibit ``bag-of-words'' behaviour, struggling on compositional benchmarks that require a strict understanding of spatial layout \cite{aro}. By combining our classical quantum-inspired image model with an existing quantum-inspired text model, we yield the first fully quantum-inspired vision-language model.

The aims of this project are threefold: (i) to explore viable quantum-inspired image encoder architectures using tensor networks; (ii) to benchmark the parameter efficiency and compositional capabilities of the quantum unitary node against classical unconstrained nodes and MLPs; and (iii) to integrate this image encoder into a multimodal framework to assess whether a structurally constrained architecture can overcome the compositional limitations of standard contrastive VLMs.

\chapter{Related Work}

\section{Language}
Historically, natural language processing has struggled to balance the continuous semantic representation of individual words with the strict structural rules that bind them together. Early approaches, such as Bag-of-Words \cite{bow} and TF-IDF \cite{tfidf}, prioritised vocabulary presence over structure, representing text purely by word frequency. While computationally cheap, these methods completely fail at compositionality; they yield identical representations for ``the man biting the dog'' and ``the dog biting the man''. The subsequent introduction of static word embeddings like Word2Vec \cite{word2vec} and GloVe \cite{glove} successfully captured distributional semantics, mapping words to dense vectors where spatial distance denotes semantic similarity. However, composing full sentences typically relied on rudimentary operations like vector addition or averaging, flattening the text and destroying the hierarchical grammar necessary for complex relational meaning.

To capture word order, sequential architectures like Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) \cite{rnn} and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) \cite{lstm} networks were introduced. While these models capture the one-dimensional sequential nature of text, they create a severe information bottleneck by compressing an arbitrarily long sentence into a single, fixed-size hidden state. Furthermore, they process text strictly from left to right rather than relationally, failing to inherently model the hierarchical tree structures of human language. This bottleneck was bypassed by the Transformer architecture \cite{attention}, which discarded recurrence entirely in favour of parallelised global self-attention. However, self-attention introduces a different compositional failure: it treats the input sequence as a fully connected graph rather than a strict linguistic hierarchy. Lacking built-in relational rules for syntax, Transformers must learn compositionality from scale, through large parameter counts and large training corpora.

In contrast to these bottom-up, data-driven approaches, computational linguistics offers a top-down paradigm that guarantees structural hierarchy by design. The Distributional Compositional Categorical (DisCoCat) model \cite{discocat} unified continuous distributional semantics with formal compositional logic trees. Grounded in applied category theory, DisCoCat maps the syntactic types of Pregroup algebra \cite{pregroup} directly onto continuous vector spaces. In this framework, words are no longer uniform vectors; their tensor order is strictly dictated by their grammatical function. The meaning of a sentence is computed by contracting these tensors along their shared grammatical indices.

This approach achieves text compositionality, but a word's tensor order follows its grammatical arity, so the words doing the compositional work carry the largest tensors. The same shape of problem appears in many-body physics, where a state over many sites is written not as one enormous tensor but as a network of local ones. Each tensor is attached to a site and each linked to its neighbours by bond indices whose dimension caps the correlation crossing them \cite{mps_physics}. A DisCoCat sentence has the same form: the words are the local tensors, the grammatical wires are the bonds, and their dimension governs both the cost of the model and how much of each constituent's meaning reaches the rest of the sentence.

\section{Vision}

Computer vision models have historically struggled to capture precise part-whole structural relationships, often settling for a ``bag of features'' approach rather than compositional understanding. Early Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs) \cite{mlp} treated images as unstructured data, requiring a flattening into a 1D vector that destroyed the 2D topological structure and spatial proximity of the pixels. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) \cite{cnn} addressed this by introducing local receptive fields to extract hierarchical features. However, CNNs rely heavily on downsampling mechanisms like max-pooling. While pooling introduces translation invariance, it discards the precise relative positions of features within the pooling window. This spatial invariance causes CNNs to act as a loose collection of local features, failing at part-whole compositional tasks (the ``Picasso effect'', where a CNN might classify a face even if the features are spatially scrambled).

To bypass the limitations of convolutions, the Vision Transformer (ViT) \cite{vit} adapted the NLP self-attention mechanism for image data. A ViT divides an image into a sequence of flattened patches and applies global self-attention. Because this operation treats the input as a 1D sequence of tokens, the ViT completely discards the native 2D grid, relying entirely on learned positional embeddings to retain spatial context. Much like text Transformers, ViTs lack built-in relational rules for 2D data. By treating image patches as a fully connected graph, they are forced to brute-force the understanding of spatial relationships through massive parameter counts and vast datasets.

In the multimodal domain, the evolution of Vision Language Models (VLMs) has centred on mapping these visual representations to language. Early architectures, such as ViLBERT \cite{vilbert} and LXMERT \cite{lxmert}, relied on computationally heavy pipelines using off-the-shelf object detectors to extract bounding boxes. This bounded the models to predefined vocabularies and destroyed the holistic 2D spatial context of the image. OpenAI's CLIP \cite{clip} eliminated bounding boxes in favour of aligning independent text and image encoders using a global contrastive InfoNCE loss \cite{infonce}. While highly scalable, this global alignment introduces the vision ``bag-of-words'' problem \cite{aro}. Because CLIP relies entirely on global feature alignment without any inherent structural inductive bias, it maps global visual features to global text vocabulary while remaining functionally blind to spatial layout. Consequently, the model fails to distinguish between text like ``a glass on a table'' and ``a table on a glass'', performing at near-chance levels on compositional benchmarks.

This asymmetry between compositional text models and non-hierarchical vision models is what the DisCoClip baseline \cite{discoclip} exposes. DisCoClip extended the DisCoCat language framework into a multimodal setting by grafting it onto a frozen CLIP vision backbone. While this provided a parameter-efficient and structurally constrained text engine, the framework's end-to-end compositional reasoning was bottlenecked by CLIP's spatial limitations. Relational understanding therefore requires replacing the spatially invariant vision encoder with an architecture that preserves 2D compositional hierarchies.

\section{Quantum Tensor Networks and VQCs}

While tensor networks, such as the Matrix Product States used in NLP and the Tree Tensor Networks adapted for vision, provide solutions to the compositionality bottleneck in classical machine learning, their origins lie in many-body quantum physics \cite{mps_physics}. Because they were fundamentally designed to model quantum entanglement, tensor networks possess a native correspondence with quantum computing.

Tensor networks entered machine learning as classical models. Stoudenmire and Schwab optimised a matrix product state directly as a supervised image classifier, sweeping its tensors with a DMRG-style update and demonstrating that a structure built for many-body states trains perfectly well by gradient descent on labelled data \cite{stoudenmire2016}. Tree topologies were subsequently applied to images specifically, on the grounds that a two-dimensional contraction order matches the spatial hierarchy of the input better than a one-dimensional chain \cite{ttn_images}. Huggins et al.\ then made the correspondence to quantum circuits explicit: an MPS or tree network of bounded bond dimension can be realised as a discretely parameterised circuit whose qubit count is set by the bond rather than by the size of the system it describes \cite{huggins2019tn}. That mapping is the one this work relies on, and it is what fixes our bond dimension at $\chi = 2$ once a single qubit is spent per bond.

In a classical tensor network, each node contracts its inputs through an unconstrained multilinear map. Written out in full, a node combining four children would be an order-five tensor, so in practice the map is parameterised in canonical polyadic (CP) form: each child is projected onto $R$ components, the projections are multiplied elementwise, and the result is projected to the output, with $R$ setting the node's capacity. Constraining that map to be unitary ($U^{\dagger}U = I$) turns the network into a Variational Quantum Circuit, and a classical architecture can then be transpiled natively into a circuit in which contractions become rotation and entangling gates. The topology is unchanged; what differs is the operation each node is permitted to perform.

Hierarchical circuits of exactly this form have been studied as classifiers in their own right. Grant et al.\ implemented tree and MERA-like circuits on both simulators and superconducting hardware, discarding the majority of qubits at each level and reading a single output wire \cite{grant2018hierarchical}; that single-wire readout is the baseline protocol we take up in Section \ref{sec:readout_protocols} and find wanting. The same log-depth coarse-graining appears in the quantum convolutional neural network of Cong et al.\ \cite{cong2019qcnn}, whose alternating convolution and pooling layers are a MERA read in reverse, and it is for this family that the absence of barren plateaus is proved \cite{pesah2021_qcnn}.

This allows the same architectural topology to be evaluated across both classical and quantum paradigms. By comparing an unconstrained classical tensor network against a unitary-constrained quantum circuit, we can isolate the effect of the quantum paradigm on parameter efficiency and expressive capacity, answering whether the unitary constraint acts as a handicap or a powerful structural regulariser.


\chapter{Theoretical Framework and Candidate Architectures}
\label{ch:theory}

\section{Universal Quantum Circuit Mechanics}

Unlike classical neural networks, which natively process and propagate continuous real-valued matrices, quantum machine learning models operate within a complex Hilbert space. As a result, all quantum machine learning models operating on classical data need to select a way to encode this information onto quantum states as well as the foundational components of the variational quantum circuits.

\subsection{Data Encoding Strategies}
Before quantum computation can occur, classical information must be mapped into a quantum state. This dictates how effectively the model can utilise the Hilbert space.

\subsubsection{Amplitude Encoding}
Amplitude Encoding \cite{amplitude_encoding} maps an entire vector of normalised classical data $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{2^N}$ directly into the complex amplitudes of an $N$-qubit quantum state:
\begin{equation}
    |\psi(\mathbf{x})\rangle = \sum_{i=0}^{2^N-1} x_i |i\rangle \quad \text{where} \quad \sum |x_i|^2 = 1
\end{equation}
While this provides an optimal, exponential compression of classical data into the quantum Hilbert space, its physical implementation requires deep, dense circuits that are prohibitive in NISQ devices.

\subsubsection{Scalar Rotation Encoding (\texttt{scalar\_ry})}
\label{sec:enc_scalar_ry}
An alternative is angle embedding \cite{angle_embedding}, which embeds a classical data point $x_i$ into the amplitude of a single qubit state by applying a Pauli-Y rotation:
\begin{equation}
    |\psi(x_i)\rangle = R_y(x_i) |0\rangle = \cos\left(\frac{x_i}{2}\right)|0\rangle + \sin\left(\frac{x_i}{2}\right)|1\rangle
\end{equation}
While highly gate-efficient, this approach confines the encoded state strictly to a single plane of the Bloch sphere, significantly underutilising the qubit's available state space. This encoding is referred to as \texttt{scalar\_ry} throughout; it appears under the alias \texttt{angle} in the single-node survey code.

\subsubsection{Multi-Axis Encoding (\texttt{multi\_axis})}
\label{sec:enc_multi_axis}
To increase representational capacity without increasing the qubit count, we evaluate a multi-axis encoding strategy. This approach distributes the classical feature across multiple rotational axes sequentially:
\begin{equation}
    |\psi(x_i)\rangle = R_z(x_i) R_y(x_i) R_x(x_i) |0\rangle
\end{equation}
By spreading the rotation over all three axes, multi-axis encoding places the data anywhere on the Bloch sphere rather than on a single plane of it, before any entanglement is applied.

\subsubsection{Second-Order Pauli-Z Feature Map (\texttt{zz\_map})}
\label{sec:enc_zz_map}
The three strategies above are all product encodings: each classical feature is written onto its own qubit independently, so the encoded state carries no correlation between features before the ansatz acts. To test whether entangling the data \textit{at encoding time} is worthwhile, we additionally evaluate the second-order Pauli-Z feature map of Havlíček et al.\ \cite{havlicek2019}. Hadamards prepare a uniform superposition, single-qubit $R_z$ rotations write each feature, and a ring of $ZZ$ couplings writes the pairwise products:
\begin{equation}
    |\psi(\mathbf{x})\rangle = \left[ \prod_{i} \text{IsingZZ}\big(x_i x_{i+1}\big) \right] \left[ \bigotimes_{i} R_z(x_i) \right] H^{\otimes N} |0\rangle^{\otimes N}
\end{equation}
where the product runs over adjacent wire pairs on a ring. The resulting state is entangled before the variational layer is applied, which raises the expressibility of the embedding but also makes it the most gate-expensive of the four.

\subsection{Parameterised Ansatz Design}
\label{sec:ansatz_design}
Once the data is encoded, the node must process it to extract relational features. This is achieved by applying a parameterised unitary operation $U(\theta)$, known as the ansatz. The structural design of the ansatz acts as the model's inductive bias.

\subsubsection{Hardware Efficient Ansatz (HEA)}
A standard baseline in variational quantum machine learning is the Hardware Efficient Ansatz (HEA) \cite{kandala2017}. This architecture applies successive layers of arbitrary single-qubit rotations followed by heavily connected entanglement gates (such as a ring of CNOTs). The unitary for a single layer $l$ operating on $N$ qubits is expressed as:
\begin{equation}
    U_{HEA}(\theta_l) = W_{ent} \prod_{j=1}^N R(\theta_{j,l})
\end{equation}
where $R$ represents parameterised $SU(2)$ rotations and $W_{ent}$ is the multi-qubit entangling array. Deep HEA circuits are expressive but susceptible to barren plateaus.

Two concrete instantiations of this family appear in the experiments that follow. The broad single-node survey uses a \textit{star-entangler} variant, in which an $R_z$--$R_y$--$R_z$ rotation triple on each wire is followed by CNOTs from every child wire onto a single parent wire; this is the arm labelled \texttt{hea}. The ranked hybrid-tree evaluation instead uses PennyLane's \texttt{StronglyEntanglingLayers}, the canonical ring-entangler realisation, and is the arm labelled \texttt{strongly\_entangling}. Both are hardware-efficient in the sense defined above and differ only in entanglement topology.

\subsubsection{Alternating Layered Ansatz (ALT)}
To reduce the CNOT gate depth and explore different trainability profiles, we evaluate an Alternating Layered Ansatz (ALT). This architecture applies layers of parameterised Pauli-Y rotations followed by a ``brickwork'' entanglement structure, where CNOT gates alternate their connectivity at each layer:
\begin{equation}
    U_{ALT}(\theta_l) = W_{brickwork}^{(l)} \prod_{j=1}^N R_y(\theta_{j,l})
\end{equation}
By restricting the rotation to a single axis and alternating the entanglement topology, ALT aims to maintain relational capacity while operating with a shallower, more noise-resilient circuit depth than HEA.

\subsubsection{Instantaneous Quantum Polynomial (IQP) Ansatz}
We additionally evaluate the Instantaneous Quantum Polynomial (IQP) ansatz \cite{iqp_ansatz, havlicek2019}. Consisting of Hadamard gates surrounding a diagonal parameterised unitary $D(\theta)$:
\begin{equation}
    U_{IQP}(\theta) = H^{\otimes N} D(\theta) H^{\otimes N}
\end{equation}
where $D(\theta)$ is typically an Ising-like Hamiltonian consisting of $Z$ rotations and $ZZ$ coupling gates. Because the core operations commute and are diagonal, the IQP ansatz possesses a strict, non-arbitrary inductive bias, which makes it more resilient to barren plateaus.


\section{Candidate Architectures}
To isolate the effects of the quantum unitary constraint on parameter efficiency and expressive capacity, we constructed three topologically matched arms alongside a non-hierarchical baseline.

\subsection{The Quantum Coherent Tree (QTTN)}
The primary architecture under investigation is the Quantum Tree Tensor Network (QTTN). It operates on a 16-qubit device structured as a hierarchical quad-tree: 16 image patches are encoded into the leaves, contracted into 4 parent nodes, and finally contracted into a single root. The qubit count is set by the number of \textit{patches}, not by the image resolution: the input is always divided into a $4 \times 4$ grid, so a larger image yields larger patches rather than more qubits, and the circuit width is unchanged. Resolution therefore scales the classical patch-embedding layer that produces the rotation angles, while the simulation cost depends only on the 16 wires. The network applies parameterised unitary gates at each node providing a parameter-efficient framework with depth scaling logarithmically with the image size for a fixed patch size. Additional qubits could be spent in either of two ways. Widening the grid gives more, smaller patches and a deeper tree, which increases spatial resolution but worsens the compression ratio, since the layer entering the root remains four qubits at any depth. Thickening the bonds between levels instead raises the bond dimension to $\chi = 2^k$ for $k$ qubits per bond, which increases the information the tree can carry upward.

\subsection{The Classical Control (CP-TTN)}
The \texttt{classical\_bare} model serves as a faithful classical analogue to the quantum model. This model shares the exact same 16-to-4-to-1 quad-tree topology as the QTTN, but replaces the unitary quantum nodes with unconstrained classical Canonical Polyadic (CP)\cite{cp_decomposition} tensor contractions. By matching the topology, we can directly compare classical unconstrained capacity against quantum constrained capacity.

\subsection{The Classical Full Model}
While the \texttt{classical\_bare} arm matches the quantum constraints, standard classical architectures typically employ mechanisms like residual connections and dropout to prevent overfitting and vanishing gradients. The quantum equivalents of these components are evaluated. We introduced a \texttt{classical\_full} arm that includes them to quantify exactly how much of a performance gap is attributable to these standard classical regularisers.

\subsection{The MLP Reference}
Interpreting any of the above requires calibrating task difficulty. We introduced a standard, non-hierarchical Multi-Layer Perceptron (\texttt{mlp\_reference}). This model is not a tensor network and serves only to benchmark the dataset. If the MLP scores highly while the tensor networks fail, it isolates the failure to the topological design rather than classical computation itself.

\subsection{Simulation Constraints and the Density Matrix Wall}
\label{sec:density_wall}
Before proceeding to optimisation, it is important to mention the computational limits that dictate what experiments are possible. To simulate realistic quantum noise, frameworks like PennyLane must compute the full density matrix, an operation that scales as $\mathcal{O}(4^N)$. For our 16-qubit coherent circuit, a noisy simulation using \texttt{default.mixed} requires approximately 68GB of memory per instance.
At this scale, batched gradient descent becomes computationally intractable. This limitation forced the abandonment of full-tree noisy simulation during training, and with it the rejection of spatial ancillas, since adding even a single ancilla qubit exponentially worsened the memory bottleneck. The architecture and experiments were therefore bounded to noiseless statevector simulation, which scales at $\mathcal{O}(2^N)$, using PennyLane's \texttt{default.qubit} and \texttt{lightning.qubit} devices. Spatial ancillas and noisy simulations were run on individual nodes of the tree, which allowed us to gauge some of the properties.

% ---------------------------------------------------------
\section{Topology-Specific Mechanics: QTTN Readout Protocols}
\label{sec:readout_protocols}
The final component required to fully define the Quantum Coherent Tree is the classical readout mechanism. Unlike 1D Matrix Product States, where information flows sequentially, a Tree Tensor Network traces out the majority of its qubits at each hierarchical layer. In our 16-patch quad-tree, 12 leaf qubits are traced out in the first layer, leaving 4 parent qubits, three of which are in turn traced out into the root.
As a result, all spatial information and features must funnel into this final root node before measurement. Extracting a classical prediction from these surviving qubits is non-trivial.

\subsection{Single Marginal Readout}
The most basic protocol, and the one used by the hierarchical quantum classifiers of Grant et al.\ \cite{grant2018hierarchical}, involves taking the expectation value of a single marginal qubit from the root node (e.g., measuring the Pauli-Z observable on wire 0, $\langle Z_0 \rangle$). While this is highly resource-efficient and minimises the number of required measurement shots on physical hardware, it forces the network to compress its entire hierarchical analysis into a single scalar expectation value, reducing the information bandwidth available to the loss function.

\subsection{Widening the Readout}
There are two distinct ways to extract more than a single number, and the difference between them matters.

The first measures additional observables on the \emph{same} qubit: the root's full Bloch vector $\langle X_0 \rangle, \langle Y_0 \rangle, \langle Z_0 \rangle$ rather than $\langle Z_0 \rangle$ alone. This returns three numbers instead of one, but all three describe the same reduced state, and the reduced state of a single qubit carries three real parameters however much structure feeds into it.

The second measures the same observable on \emph{more} qubits: $\langle Z \rangle$ on each of the four wires entering the top node, read after that node has acted, so the root is measured together with the three qubits the tree would otherwise discard. These are marginals of different parts of the register, and jointly they carry far more about the 16-qubit state. Both protocols feed their outputs into a small classical dense layer.

The distinction yields a testable prediction. If the constraint is simply how many numbers reach the classical head, both widenings should help. If it is the bond dimension, only the second should.

\subsection{Bond Dimension and the Multi-Wire Workaround}
In tensor network theory, the expressivity and information bandwidth of the network are fundamentally governed by the \textit{bond dimension} ($\chi$) \cite{schollwock2011}. The bond dimension restricts the maximum entanglement entropy that can be shared across any bipartite split of the network. Mathematically, for a state bipartitioned into subsystems $A$ and $B$, the Schmidt decomposition is bounded by $\chi$:
\begin{equation}
    |\Psi\rangle = \sum_{\alpha=1}^{\chi} \lambda_\alpha |\phi_\alpha^{[A]}\rangle \otimes |\phi_\alpha^{[B]}\rangle
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_\alpha$ are the Schmidt coefficients. In a classical tensor network, $\chi$ can be arbitrarily scaled up by expanding the tensor index dimensions. In a quantum circuit the bond dimension is determined by the number of qubits, $n$, forming the connection between a child node and its parent.

In our simulated 16-qubit architecture, the inter-node connection is restricted to a single qubit ($n=1$), meaning the state space passed up the tree is severely bottlenecked to a bond dimension of $\chi = 2^n =2$.

This is why the two widenings are predicted to behave differently. Reading more observables from the root cannot recover what the bond never carried: the root qubit's reduced state is fixed at three real parameters regardless of the structure below it. Reading the three qubits the tree discards at the top does recover something, because those qubits were never squeezed through the final bond.

The multi-wire protocol is therefore a workaround for a simulation limit rather than a design choice. It widens the effective bond dimension only at the very top of the tree. Were a larger bond affordable (passing $k$ qubits per bond, giving $\chi = 2^k$) the tree would carry the information upward properly and a reading of the root register alone would suffice.


\chapter{Topological Optimisation}
\label{ch:synthetic}

\section{Dataset and Aims}

To evaluate the architectures of Chapter \ref{ch:theory} we use a procedurally generated Synthetic Shapes dataset of $16 \times 16$ RGB images. The four classes form a $2 \times 2$ design over $\{$red, green$\}\times\{$circle, square$\}$, so colour and shape are independent. Shapes are jittered in position and scale and carry noise to discourage position memorisation. The generation code is given in Appendix \ref{appendix:synthetic_data}.

Chance is $25\%$. Colour alone splits the four classes into two pairs, and shape alone into two different pairs, so either attribute in isolation caps at $50\%$. The $50\%$ faux-ceiling is the line to watch: only a model using both attributes together can pass it.

The primary aim of this phase is to establish a perception baseline and subject the candidate quantum topologies to empirical trainability tests. A common issue in Variational Quantum Circuits is the barren plateau phenomenon \cite{barren_plateaus}, where gradients vanish exponentially as a function of qubit count and circuit depth. Before advancing to complex, multi-object datasets, we must verify whether the structural design of the candidate topologies mitigates this issue.

This controlled, low-resolution environment allows us to rapidly iterate and execute four evaluations:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Trainability:} Measuring gradient variance across topologies and qubit counts, to establish whether gradients survive at the widths used.
    \item \textbf{Node Design:} Sweeping encoding strategies against variational ans\"{a}tze to select the node's internal mechanics.
    \item \textbf{Readout Width:} Establishing how much of the tree's final state reaches the classical head, and what limits it.
    \item \textbf{Classical Mechanisms:} Testing whether dropout, residual connections and explicit positional encoding transfer to a quantum node, and how the node behaves under simulated noise.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Experimental Methodology}
To execute the four evaluations outlined above, we use an elimination-based testing protocol. The configurations defined in Chapter \ref{ch:theory} are swept across the Synthetic Shapes dataset. Any architectural combination that triggers exponential gradient decay, fails to converge beyond random chance, or needs more parameters than can be reasonably simulated is deemed non-viable and eliminated.

All candidate models are trained using the Adam optimiser with a categorical cross-entropy loss function. To ensure a fair, best-case comparison, hyperparameters such as learning rates and weight initialisation bounds are independently tuned for each architecture.

Due to the $\mathcal{O}(4^N)$ memory limits of noisy emulation (the Density Matrix Wall established in Section \ref{sec:density_wall}), all quantum training is executed via exact statevector simulation using PennyLane's \texttt{lightning.qubit} device. This bypasses the memory bottleneck while maintaining mathematical fidelity for the unitary operations.

\subsection{Reporting Comparisons}
Every arm is run at several random initialisations, and each run is scored on the mean of its final five epochs rather than a single final value, which removes most of the epoch-to-epoch noise. An arm is therefore summarised by a mean and a spread across seeds.

Comparing two arms means asking whether the gap between their means is larger than that spread can explain. Because arms differ in both variance and seed count, we use Welch's unequal-variance form to compute the smallest difference the design could distinguish from zero. The minimum detectable effect is reported throughout as the \textit{limit}. A difference exceeding its limit is \textit{resolved}; one falling below it is not, and the two arms are reported as statistically tied. An unresolved comparison is not evidence that two arms perform identically, only that this experiment could not separate them, with the limit stating how large a difference would have been needed.

\section{Optimisation Results}

\subsection{Barren Plateau Investigation}
In unstructured quantum neural networks, gradient variance decays exponentially with respect to the number of qubits rendering models functionally untrainable.

To empirically diagnose whether our candidate tensor network architectures suffer from exponential gradient decay at operational depths, we executed a variance scaling sweep up to $N=20$ qubits. For the 1D Matrix Product State (MPS), Multi-scale Entanglement Renormalization Ansatz (MERA), and our Quantum Tree Tensor Network (QTTN), we initialised the models randomly and computed the variance of the root observable's gradient with respect to the deepest leaf parameter across 100 trials, that is, how far the gradient signal decays as it propagates back to the deepest layer of the model.

As shown in Figure \ref{fig:topology_barren_plateaus}, the empirical variance did not decay exponentially for any of the networks. Instead, all three topologies closely tracked a slow, polynomial decay.

\begin{figure}[hbt!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{ch3/topology_barren_plateaus.png}
    \caption{Variance of the root observable's gradient with respect to the deepest leaf parameter, as a function of qubit count, for the MPS, MERA and QTTN topologies. 100 random initialisations per point, swept to $N = 20$.}
    \label{fig:topology_barren_plateaus}
\end{figure}

For the QTTN, this empirical success is theoretically supported. Literature shows that hierarchical topologies avoid barren plateaus: Pesah et al.\ prove it for the quantum convolutional network of Cong et al.\ \cite{cong2019qcnn, pesah2021_qcnn}, and Liu et al.\ for tree tensor networks under local cost functions \cite{liu2022tnbp}. The latter condition applies here, since our readout is a set of single-wire Pauli-$Z$ marginals rather than a global observable; barren plateau behaviour is cost-function dependent, and a global measurement over the same circuit would not inherit the guarantee \cite{cerezo2021cost}. However, the proof does not extend to 1D MPS or standard MERA networks. Their survival in this specific diagnostic is an empirical artifact of the shallow depths and bounded scales ($N \le 20$) tested. Nonetheless, at the operational scales required for our vision task, the gradient variance of all architectures remained stable.


\subsection{Ansatz and Encoding Investigation}

Having established the trainability of the QTTN topology, the next phase of optimisation focused on its internal node mechanics.

\subsubsection{Documenting the Search Space}
\label{sec:search_space}
To explore the available configuration space, we executed a combinatorial sweep across the four encoding strategies defined in Section \ref{sec:enc_scalar_ry}--\ref{sec:enc_zz_map} (\texttt{scalar\_ry}, \texttt{multi\_axis}, \texttt{amplitude} and \texttt{zz\_map}) and the three ans\"{a}tze of Section \ref{sec:ansatz_design} (HEA, ALT and IQP), giving twelve configurations. The results are shown in Figure \ref{fig:encoding_ansatz_survey}.

The diagnostic is constrained in one respect; it was executed on a downsampled $8 \times 8$ dataset using a single 4-qubit quantum node rather than the full hierarchical tree, so it characterises node-level circuits and not the tower.

\begin{figure}[hbt!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{ch3/encoding_ansatz_survey.png}
    \caption{Node-level survey of the search space: four encodings $\times$ three ans\"{a}tze on a single 4-qubit node at $8 \times 8$, 30 seeds per configuration, noiseless. The dashed line marks the $50\%$ single-attribute ceiling.}
    \label{fig:encoding_ansatz_survey}
\end{figure}

Three observations follow. The \texttt{multi\_axis} encoding leads at every ansatz, reaching $64.0\%$ against $59.6\%$ for \texttt{scalar\_ry}, $56.5\%$ for \texttt{zz\_map} and $52.3\%$ for \texttt{amplitude}.

The \texttt{amplitude} encoding, by contrast, sits at $51.5$--$52.3\%$ across all three ans\"{a}tze, ie exactly at the single-attribute ceiling. Compressing four patches into two qubits is the most qubit-efficient option available, but at this width the encoding does not learn the colour--shape pairing at all; it resolves one attribute and stops. \texttt{Amplitude} is eliminated here rather than carried forward.

The ansatz, however, is \textit{not} resolved by this survey. HEA and IQP are statistically tied at every encoding, differing by $0.7$ points at \texttt{multi\_axis} against a resolution limit of $1.7$. ALT is the only arm that loses resolvably and is not carried forward. The two surviving ans\"{a}tze were therefore taken to a stricter evaluation at the full image resolution, on the full tree.

\subsubsection{Hybrid-Tree Evaluation}
\label{sec:hybrid_eval}
To determine the optimal configuration, the surviving candidates from the survey were subjected to an evaluation using 21 independent random seeds on the $16 \times 16$ dataset (1024 train / 64 test). At this scale the hardware-efficient arm is PennyLane's standard \texttt{StronglyEntanglingLayers}, denoted \texttt{strongly\_entangling}, in preference to the hand-rolled node circuit used in the survey; the two are members of the same family but are not identical circuits.

It should be noted that this ranking was established using a \textit{hybrid} tree architecture (the measure-and-re-encode topology). In this framework, every node builds its own 4-qubit device, and the levels communicate by passing classical scalar measurements, completely bypassing the need for a massive coherent 16-qubit register. Statistical significance across the configurations was determined using Welch's unequal variances t-test to calculate the Minimum Detectable Effect (MDE).

As shown in Figure \ref{fig:encoding_ansatz}, moving from \texttt{scalar\_ry} (113 parameters) to \texttt{multi\_axis} (211 parameters) yields $+12.9$ points on the hybrid tree, against a resolution limit of $3.7$. The node-level survey put the same gap at $4.4$ points, so the encoding's advantage roughly triples once sixteen patches are contracted through the tree: a difference in how faithfully each patch is written onto its qubit compounds across contraction levels rather than washing out.

\begin{figure}[hbt!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{ch3/encoding_ansatz.png}
    \caption{Encoding and ansatz configuration sweep on the hybrid tree ($16 \times 16$ images), 21 seeds per configuration.}
    \label{fig:encoding_ansatz}
\end{figure}

With the encoding re-established, we evaluated the parameterised ansatz, comparing the \texttt{strongly\_entangling} baseline against the structured \texttt{iqp} ansatz. The result reproduces the survey's verdict: the two ans\"{a}tze are statistically tied. The IQP ansatz achieved a mean accuracy of $80.9\%$, edging out the strongly entangling ansatz at $79.3\%$, but as shown in Figure \ref{fig:ansatz_comparison} this $+1.6$ point difference falls strictly below the statistical resolution limit ($\text{MDE} = 4.8$).

\begin{figure}[hbt!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{ch3/ansatz_comparison.png}
    \caption{Per-seed spread for the \texttt{strongly\_entangling} and \texttt{iqp} ans\"{a}tze on the hybrid tree, 21 seeds each.}
    \label{fig:ansatz_comparison}
\end{figure}

Despite the statistical tie, the \texttt{iqp} ansatz was selected for two structural reasons. First, it demonstrated a tighter spread across the 21 seeds (standard deviation of $4.9$ vs $9.7$), indicating more stable training. Second, the IQP circuit uses commuting, diagonal operations, which require a shallower physical gate depth.

This winning node configuration (\texttt{multi\_axis} encoding and \texttt{iqp} ansatz) was adopted and ported directly into the fully coherent 16-qubit QTTN (evaluated later in this chapter) as the architecture of record.

\subsection{Solving the Readout Bottleneck}

Section \ref{sec:readout_protocols} predicted that widening the readout would help only if it drew on qubits the tree discards, and not if it merely returned more numbers from the root. The three protocols were compared directly on the coherent tree.

As shown in Figure \ref{fig:readout_bottleneck}, the prediction holds. Reading $\langle Z_0 \rangle$ alone gives $34.1\%$; widening to the root's full Bloch vector gives $35.6\%$, essentially nothing for triple the values; reading $\langle Z \rangle$ on all four top-layer wires gives $78.4\%$. The 43-point gap is therefore not a question of how many numbers reach the classical head, but of where they are read from. Four marginals drawn from different parts of the register carry far more about the 16-qubit state than three parameters of a single qubit's reduced state.

\begin{figure}[hbt!]
    \centering
    % Insert the readout bottleneck / multi-pauli comparison figure here
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{ch3/readout_bottleneck.png}
    \caption{The three readout protocols of Section \ref{sec:readout_protocols} compared on the coherent tree: the single marginal $\langle Z_0 \rangle$, the root's full Bloch vector $\langle X_0 \rangle, \langle Y_0 \rangle, \langle Z_0 \rangle$, and $\langle Z \rangle$ on each of the four top-layer wires.}
    \label{fig:readout_bottleneck}
\end{figure}

This is a workaround for a simulation limit. Reading the discarded qubits widens the effective bond dimension only at the very top of the tree; a physical device able to pass more than one qubit per bond would carry the information upward properly and could be read from its root alone.

\subsection{Dropout and Residuals: Porting Classical Mechanisms}
\label{sec:ablations}

With the baseline node architecture resolved, we conducted a final series of evaluations to determine if we could port highly successful mechanisms from classical Deep Learning directly into the quantum tensor network. Specifically, we investigated quantum analogs for dropout regularisation, residual skip connections, and explicit positional embeddings.

We evaluated the following node-level modifications:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Quantum Dropout via Depolarising Noise:} In classical networks, Dropout \cite{srivastava2014dropout} prevents overfitting by randomly nullifying activations. We subjected our architectures to varying depolarising noise channels ($p$) to determine if quantum noise could act as a structural equivalent to Dropout.
    \item \textbf{Classical Residuals (\texttt{mixed\_channel}):} Inspired by ResNets \cite{he2016resnet}, this mechanism attempts to port residual skip connections into the quantum domain by allowing certain classical feature channels to bypass the quantum node entirely, preserving classical gradients.
    \item \textbf{Spatial Ancillae (\texttt{with\_ancilla}):} Allocating an auxiliary qubit to explicitly encode spatial positional embeddings.
    \item \textbf{Data Re-uploading (\texttt{reupload}):} Re-injecting classical features deep into the ansatz to artificially increase non-linearity, following the protocol established by Pérez-Salinas et al. \cite{perez_salinas_2020}.
\end{enumerate}

These ablations were run on the hybrid tree across 30 random seeds. That architecture is what makes the noise sweep possible at all: because each node is its own four-qubit device and the levels communicate through classical measurements, no more than four qubits are ever simulated at once. The coherent tree holds all sixteen simultaneously, where the same density matrix needs $4^{16}$ entries. The noise results therefore characterise a single node rather than the assembled tower.

The empirical results, shown in Figure \ref{fig:ablations_and_noise}, rejected the translation of these classical mechanisms into the quantum architecture.

\begin{figure}[hbt!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{ch3/ablations_and_noise.png}
    \caption{Node-level ablations and depolarising-noise sweeps, measured on the hybrid tree at single-node scale --- full-tree emulation of noise is infeasible at $\mathcal{O}(4^N)$. 30 seeds per arm. The ancilla arm is measured on a translation-invariant task, where position barely affects the label; the relational case is tested separately in Chapter \ref{ch:clevr}.}
    \label{fig:ablations_and_noise}
\end{figure}

The \texttt{reupload} mechanism achieved a nominal $+1.5$ point increase over the baseline; however, this fell well below the statistical resolution limit ($\text{MDE} = 3.8$), failing to justify its additional gate depth.
The \texttt{mixed\_channel} approach, our attempt to port residual connections, harmed performance. The \texttt{with\_ancilla} positional embedding cost $-19.5$ points. Because the Synthetic Shapes dataset evaluates a largely translation-invariant classification task, explicitly encoding positional data into a spatial ancilla provided no predictive benefit, while each ancilla qubit doubles the required statevector dimension.
Finally, the noise degradation sweep revealed that the plain \texttt{baseline} architecture was more resilient to depolarising channels than any of the alternatives. Classical residuals and spatial ancillae were therefore discarded: the baseline node was both the most accurate and the most noise-resilient arm tested.

\chapter{CLEVR: Relational and Compositional Evaluation}
\label{ch:clevr}

Having established a baseline on synthetic shapes, we now use the CLEVR dataset \cite{johnson2017clevr} to probe visual reasoning, providing complex 3D scenes populated with multiple interacting objects. Each object exhibits distinct visual attributes (colour, shape, material, size) and positioning. By deploying our surviving Quantum Tree Tensor Network (QTTN) and classical tensor controls on this dataset, we aim to answer whether quantum tensor topologies can naturally encode spatial geometry and resolve compositional binding better than unconstrained classical alternatives.

\section{Methodology and Aims}
To map the architectural limits of tensor networks, our evaluation on the CLEVR dataset is structured across an escalating hierarchy of visual reasoning. We define a three-stage progression:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Perception:} Scaling the models to extract the full 15-class attribute space to establish a benchmark in multi-object scenes.
    \item \textbf{Spatial Relations:} Evaluating the geometric ability of the quad-tree on explicit spatial routing tasks.
    \item \textbf{Compositional Binding:} Confronting the models with the binding problem \cite{greff2020binding} to test if they can structurally associate features with distinct spatial entities.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Dataset Adaptation: World-Scaled Cropping}
\label{sec:crop}
Before the models could evaluate CLEVR scenes, the data had to be processed into object crops suitable for our 16-qubit input boundary, that is, a $4 \times 4$ grid of patches. However, naive 2D bounding-box cropping introduces a problem: a physically ``large'' object and a ``small'' object will both be rescaled to fill $100\%$ of the crop window, rendering the model blind to the \texttt{size} attribute. Furthermore, perspective projection means a small object close to the camera can appear identical to a large object far away.

To resolve this, we create a depth-invariant, world-scaled cropping methodology governed by a global calibration constant, \texttt{CROP\_K}. The pixel width of the crop is calculated strictly as a function of the object's 3D distance from the camera:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Crop Side (px)} = \frac{\texttt{CROP\_K}}{\text{Depth}}
\end{equation}

By scaling the crop window inversely with depth, the crop captures a constant physical area of the 3D world. Within this fixed window, the proportion of the image filled by the object becomes a direct, depth-invariant measure of its true physical radius.

\begin{figure}[hbt!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{ch3/clevr_crop_calibration.png}
    \caption{World-scaled cropping calibration (\texttt{CROP\_K} = 1470.0). The crop side is set from the object's 3D depth rather than its 2D pixel bounds, so a physically large object (right) fills a larger fraction of the window than a small one (left).}
    \label{fig:clevr_crop}
\end{figure}

As shown in Figure \ref{fig:clevr_crop}, this approach isolates the true physical dimensions of the object, ensuring the \texttt{size} attribute remains learnable.

\subsection{Defining the Relational Task}
To evaluate the second stage of our progression, we derived explicit relational tasks from the CLEVR scenes (Left, Right, Front, Behind). Examples are given in Appendix \ref{appendix:clevr_tasks}.

This task investigates whether the implicit geometric hierarchy of the tensor network naturally encodes spatial geometry, or if explicit positional embeddings are required for spatial awareness.

\subsection{Defining the Compositional Binding Probe}
The final stage of the progression targets the binding problem. Standard neural networks often confuse which attributes belong to which object in complex scenes.

To evaluate this, we create the Compositional Binding Probe. The models are tasked with distinguishing between scenes that contain the exact same raw features, but in swapped configurations (e.g., distinguishing a [red square, blue circle] from a [blue square, red circle]); examples are given in Appendix \ref{appendix:clevr_tasks}. Because each probe image is a composite of two objects rather than a single crop, this stage is run at $32 \times 32$ rather than the $16 \times 16$ used elsewhere in this chapter. The circuit is unchanged at 16 qubits; only the patches, and hence the classical embedding layer feeding them, are larger, which is why every model has more parameters here.

If an architecture simply acts as a ``bag of features'' without the structural capacity to bind those features to distinct spatial locations, it will fail to separate these swapped configurations.

\section{Perception and the Multi-Head Architecture}

To execute the first stage of our evaluation, we established a perception baseline on the processed CLEVR images. Unlike the synthetic dataset in Chapter \ref{ch:synthetic} (which evaluated a single 4-class shape target), the CLEVR dataset requires the simultaneous classification of 15 distinct attribute classes across four independent dimensions: 8 colours, 3 shapes, 2 materials, and 2 sizes.

To achieve this, the model was upgraded to a multi-head architecture. Instead of a single linear layer, four independent linear heads, one for each attribute dimension, were attached to the final top-layer multi-Pauli measurement of the tree. All four heads draw from the same underlying quantum measurement vector, so the network must resolve all 15 classes from a single statevector rather than allocating separate capacity to each attribute.

Accommodating these additional classification heads increased the parameter count of the fully coherent Quantum Tree Tensor Network (QTTN) to 462 parameters. To ensure a capacity-matched comparison, the structurally faithful classical tensor control (\texttt{classical\_bare}) was tuned to 563 parameters and the optimised \texttt{classical\_full} baseline scaled to 551 parameters. An unstructured, 1186-parameter classical Multi-Layer Perceptron (\texttt{mlp\_reference}) was also included to establish the dataset's overall task difficulty ceiling.

On the remaining three attributes (material, size, and colour), the quantum model achieved statistical parity, with gaps falling below the resolution limit imposed by seed variance.

\section{Spatial Relations}

To evaluate the second stage of our progression, the models were tasked with a four-way spatial relation classification (Left, Right, Front, Behind) on two-object CLEVR crops. Because CLEVR defines these spatial relations against a camera-rotated basis, this task tests the network's ability to learn complex geometric transformations rather than simple axis heuristics. The floor for this classification is a 26.6\% majority-class baseline. Before evaluating the quantum architectures, classical controls established the viability of the task: an unstructured \texttt{mlp\_reference} achieved 65.4\%, and the 428-parameter \texttt{classical\_bare} tensor network 49.6\%.

Two variants of the quantum tower are compared. In \texttt{quantum\_none} no positional embedding is explicitly present. In \texttt{quantum\_on\_wire}, each patch carries two further learned rotation angles applied to its own wire, fixed per patch and independent of image content, so the wire acquires a signature identifying which patch it is. No extra qubits are required because nothing extra is stored: the same qubit is rotated to a different point. The trade is that a qubit's state has only two degrees of freedom, so position and content share them rather than occupying separate registers. This differs from the spatial ancilla rejected in Chapter \ref{ch:synthetic}, which gave position its own qubit and doubled the simulated state.

While quantum training frequently collapsed to the chance floor, on the runs that successfully converged, the 462-parameter \texttt{quantum\_none} and 494-parameter \texttt{quantum\_on\_wire} models reached 46.2\% and 48.0\% respectively, both statistically tied with the \texttt{classical\_bare} control. Because the performance gaps ($-3.4$ and $-1.6$ points) fall strictly below the minimum detectable effects ($5.3$ and $5.2$), we confirm parity between the unitary quantum node and the classical CP node. These quantum arms ran at the 30-epoch budget rather than the 90 used for perception, and several converged runs were still improving when training stopped, so they are a floor on the architecture's capability rather than a ceiling; the parity result is therefore conservative.

An important finding here is that \texttt{quantum\_none} carries no explicit positional parameters. Reaching parity without them indicates that the geometric hierarchy of the tree topology encodes spatial position on its own. This also discharges the earlier failure of the spatial ancilla in Chapter \ref{ch:synthetic}, which was measured on a translation-invariant task and gave no signal on relational reasoning. Adding explicit position on top of that does not measurably help: \texttt{quantum\_on\_wire} scores $1.8$ points higher, against a resolution limit of $5.4$. The comparison is also confounded, since \texttt{on\_wire} carries 32 more parameters, so any advantage it did show could be capacity rather than position.

\section{The Compositional Binding Probe}
\label{sec:binding_probe}

While the preceding tasks established visual competency and relational geometry, neither perception nor spatial routing is compositional. To probe composition we conduct a binding probe experiment. The model is presented with two-object CLEVR composites, where each composite contains exactly one large object and one small object. The label is simply which side the large object sits on. The architecture must bind the \texttt{size} attribute to a specific spatial coordinate to resolve the task.\footnote{The binding probe relies exclusively on the \texttt{size} attribute because the perception baseline of Table \ref{tab:perception_results} proved it is the one attribute every evaluated architecture can reliably see, at $91.8$--$98.6\%$ against a $50.6\%$ floor.}

Every classical architecture was evaluated against a patch-shuffled control. As detailed in Table \ref{tab:binding_results}, randomising the spatial order of the image patches collapses every one of them to chance. This control guarantees that a naive ``bag-of-features'' model cannot solve the probe. The classical tensor networks do bind, resolving the task at 91.5\% and 93.2\%. However, they do not bind \emph{better} than an unstructured baseline: a 683-parameter \texttt{mlp\_param\_matched} architecture reaches 94.6\% with fewer parameters than any tensor-network arm.

\begin{table}[hbt!]
    \centering

\section{The Frozen Baseline: CLIP's Binding Geometry}
\label{sec:clip_geometry}

We ran the same probe on a frozen CLIP image encoder \cite{radford2021learning}. Rather than training a classifier on its features, we looked at how far its output moves when the image changes, because comparing outputs this way, by cosine similarity, is exactly what CLIP does when it retrieves or classifies an image. Each comparison reuses the same two objects, so only their arrangement differs. Swapping the two objects between sides moves CLIP's output by $0.0050$; replacing them with different objects moves it by $0.0654$, some thirteen times further.\footnote{Composites for this measurement are built at CLIP's native $224 \times 224$ input resolution rather than the $32 \times 32$ used for the trainable arms, since a frozen encoder cannot be re-fit to a smaller input. The construction is otherwise identical.} CLIP is therefore not blind to how objects are arranged, but rearranging them registers as only $7.6\%$ of the change caused by swapping the objects themselves. On composites built the same way, a 683-parameter network tells the two arrangements apart $94.6\%$ of the time. This helps explain why such models behave like bags of words on ARO \cite{yuksekgonul2023when}.

\chapter{Vision-Language Integration}
\label{ch:aro}

Having established the compositional capabilities of tensor topologies on vision tasks, the final phase integrates the quad-tree architecture into a full Vision-Language Model (VLM) via the Attribution, Relation, and Order (ARO) and COCO benchmarks.

\section{Scaling Limits and the Classical Pivot}

Natural images carry far more structure than a $4 \times 4$ patch grid can represent, so transitioning to a full VLM requires deepening the quad-tree. The tower used here operates on $64 \times 64$ inputs divided into $4 \times 4$ pixel patches, giving a $16 \times 16$ grid of 256 patches and a tree of depth four rather than the depth-two, 16-patch tree of the preceding chapters.

As established in Chapter \ref{ch:theory}, the qubit count of the coherent architecture is set by the number of patches. A 256-patch grid therefore requires a 256-qubit input layer, and because physical quantum hardware is unavailable we would have to simulate it exactly, at $\mathcal{O}(2^N)$. Sixteen qubits is trivial; 256 is physically impossible. The coherent quantum circuit is therefore abandoned for this final phase.

We pivot instead to the classical CP-TTN, which preserves the hierarchical quad-tree topology while contracting through unconstrained CP nodes rather than unitaries. Concretely, the tower corresponds to the \texttt{classical\_full} arm rather than \texttt{classical\_bare}: it includes dropout, and residual connections on its upper two levels.

\section{The DisCoViz Architecture}

To carry compositional structure end to end in a multimodal setting, we introduce DisCoViz, which is directly inspired from DisCoCLIP \cite{discoclip}. This architecture replaces the structurally blind vision encoder of a standard VLM with our Canonical Polyadic Tree Tensor Network (CP-TTN). Pairing that 2D vision backbone with the DisCoCat language model \cite{discocat} gives an architecture in which both towers contract along an explicit structure rather than pooling globally.

Integrating the hierarchical image TTN into a shared contrastive latent space alongside a text model required solving three distinct problems: preparing patches for contraction, maintaining relational capacity during that contraction, and aligning disparate multi-modal scales.

\subsection{Bilinear Patch Embedding}
Each $4 \times 4$ patch enters the tree as a bond-dimension-64 vector. Rather than flattening colour and position into a single linear map, the embedding factorises them: one learned factor contracts the three colour channels and a second contracts the sixteen pixel positions, and the two are combined by an elementwise product. This separates \textit{what} a patch contains from \textit{where} within the patch it sits, and the multiplicative combination lets the two interact without either dominating the other at initialisation.

\subsection{The Inverted Funnel}
Each level of the tree reduces the spatial resolution by a factor of four, and at a fixed bond dimension that compression is where relational detail is lost. The vision backbone therefore widens its internal bond dimension ($D$) as it contracts, doubling at each level ($D_{out} = 2 \times D_{in}$), ie  an inverted funnel.

The $16 \times 16$ grid of 256 patches contracts over four levels while the bond dimension runs $64 \to 128 \to 256 \to 512 \to 1024$. The root tensor is therefore 1024-dimensional, and a final linear projection maps it to the 512-dimensional embedding shared with the text tower. Because the bond widens faster than the arity of each merge, no level is forced to compress four children into a space the size of one.

\subsection{Positional Encoding}
The vision tower carries a learned positional embedding, one vector per patch, added to the patch representations before contraction and scaled by a learned gate. This is a departure from the quantum tower, where positional parameters yielded no benefit.

Chapter \ref{ch:clevr} found no benefit from explicit positional encoding, but measured it on a depth-two tree over 16 patches. This tower is four levels deep over 256. Each contraction merges a patch with three siblings, so position must survive four merges rather than two and is therefore far more `smeared' by the time it reaches the root. That is why explicit position earns its place here and did not before. The gate keeps the addition safe: initialised small, the network can leave the signal near zero if the topology already suffices.

\subsection{Output Normalisation}
The two towers produced embeddings whose norms differed by orders of magnitude, which destabilised the contrastive gradients and stalled early training runs. Both are therefore L2-normalised at their projection heads, placing image and text on a shared unit sphere.

\section{The ARO Dataset and Setup}

To evaluate DisCoViz, we use the Attribution, Relation, and Order (ARO) benchmark. ARO exposes the ``bag-of-features'' vulnerabilities in standard VLMs by challenging them to distinguish true captions from structurally swapped hard negatives. We evaluate on two specific tasks: \textbf{Attribution}, requiring the model to bind adjectives to specific nouns (e.g., distinguishing ``a red door and a blue wall'' from the hard-negative distractor ``a blue door and a red wall''); and \textbf{Relation}, requiring the resolution of explicit spatial or action-based geometries (e.g., ``a horse eating grass'' vs. ``grass eating a horse'').


To optimise the network for these fine-grained distinctions, the architecture is trained using a composite loss function ($L_{total} = L_{InfoNCE} + \lambda L_{Triplet}$). The InfoNCE component maintains global latent alignment across the batch by maximising the cosine similarity ($\text{sim}$) of matching image-text pairs $(I_i, T_i)$ against all other mismatched pairs $(I_i, T_j)$:
\begin{equation}
    L_{InfoNCE} = - \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \log \frac{\exp(\text{sim}(I_i, T_i) / \tau)}{\sum_{j=1}^N \exp(\text{sim}(I_i, T_j) / \tau)}
\end{equation}
where $N$ is the batch size and $\tau$ is a fixed temperature. A learnable temperature was tried and abandoned: it collapsed towards zero during training, sharpening the softmax until the gradient signal from all but the hardest negative vanished.
Simultaneously, the Triplet loss explicitly leverages the hard negatives provided directly by the ARO dataset. For a given anchor image $I_i$, a true caption $T_i$, and a hard-negative swapped distractor $T_{neg}$, the loss is formulated as:
\begin{equation}
    L_{Triplet} = \max(0, \text{sim}(I_i, T_{neg}) - \text{sim}(I_i, T_i) + \alpha)
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$ represents the margin. By prioritising these hard-negative gradients through the $\lambda$ scaling factor, the optimiser is forced to mathematically resolve the subtle structural differences between the true captions and the specific shuffled distractors.

The baseline for this evaluation is \textbf{DisCoClip} \cite{discoclip}. Both DisCoViz and DisCoClip use the same DisCoCat language model for text embedding, so the language-processing capacity is held constant and any performance delta originates on the vision side. DisCoClip relies on a frozen, self-attention-based CLIP Vision Transformer, whereas DisCoViz uses the CP-TTN.

Two things differ between those encoders. The first is architectural: CLIP applies global self-attention over a flattened sequence of patches, while the CP-TTN contracts them hierarchically in two dimensions. The second is that CLIP's tower is \textit{frozen} while the CP-TTN is \textit{trained}. A performance delta is therefore attributable to the replacement of the encoder as a whole, and not to topology alone; separating the two would require a trained non-hierarchical tower of comparable size. What the comparison does establish cleanly is that the tensor-network tower is a viable substitute for frozen CLIP in a compositional setting. No assertions can be made of the superiority, or inferiority, of attention-based mechanisms, instead only that a frozen general-purpose encoder discards the spatial information these tasks depend on, as measured directly in Section \ref{sec:clip_geometry}.

\subsection{Non-Linear Contractions}
The text tower as described is multilinear: a sentence embedding is a product of its word tensors, and no number of contractions changes that. A multilinear map combines its arguments the same way whatever their values, which is a poor fit for ARO, where true and false captions contain the same words and differ only in which attaches to which. We therefore insert a gated GELU after each pairwise contraction,
\begin{equation}
    x' = x + \gamma \cdot \mathrm{GELU}(x),
\end{equation}
with $\gamma$ learned. This lets a constituent's contribution depend on what its neighbours turned out to be.

\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work}

\section{Conclusion}

This report set out to build the missing half of a quantum-inspired vision--language model. The language side already existed in DisCoCat, whose contraction follows a sentence's grammatical structure; the open question was whether an image encoder could be built on the same principle, and whether replacing its classical tensor nodes with genuine quantum unitaries costs or buys anything. We surveyed a family of candidate encoders: tree, matrix-product and MERA topologies across four data encodings and three ans\"{a}tze and narrowed them on measured evidence, and evaluated the survivor in isolation, on CLEVR, and finally as one tower of a full vision--language model.

Against the three aims set out in the introduction: the first is met, with a viable encoder identified and its internals resolved on measured evidence rather than assumed. The second is met with a bounded verdict, that the unitary node is not worse than its unconstrained classical counterpart at matched capacity and is better on synthetic data, but not that it is better in general. The third is met in part. Substituting the tensor tower for frozen CLIP improves both ARO tasks, but the gain is attributable to preserving spatial arrangement rather than to any compositional advantage of hierarchical contraction, and it does not survive training on general captions.

\subsection{Architecture}

The selected architecture is a coherent quantum tree tensor network. Two design decisions proved important and were established through experiments: intermediate states must pass between tree levels as qubits rather than being measured and re-encoded, and the model must read its top layer rather than a single root, because of simulation limitations. Common classical mechanisms like residual connections, dropout and explicit positional encoding, were tested and rejected due to lack of evidence supporting their usefulness.

\subsection{The Quantum Node}

The unitarity constraint is not a liability. On synthetic shapes the quantum tree node outperforms its unconstrained classical counterpart, and it ties the strongest classical tensor network in the study.

That result is weaker but not absent on real data. On CLEVR the quantum tower holds an eighteen per cent parameter deficit to the same classical counterpart and wins one of four attribute heads, tying on the other three. What can be said looking at the synthetic data and the CLEVR dataset is that the unitary node is not worse at matched capacity, but the stronger claim, that it is better, cannot be made. A secondary observation concerns multi-head training: extending the optimisation budget causes attribute heads sharing a single loss to trade against one another rather than improve together, so no single budget is optimal for all of them.

\subsection{Compositional Structure}

The aim was to establish that a quantum-native architecture can capture compositional structure, and it does. The quantum tower separates the arrangements well above chance. It does so with no positional parameters whatsoever, with position entering only through the fixed assignment of image patches to qubit wires. At this scale the tree's topology is therefore sufficient on its own: compositional information is captured by the architecture rather than supplied to it, and adding an explicit positional mechanism changes nothing measurable.

The quantum tower manages to bind, but it also displays a bimodal distribution, with the majority of runs failing to train and falling to chance, something attributed to a training failure that can be easily identified and discarded. The plain neural network also binds equally well, clearly showing that hierarchical contraction is not the only architecture that can.

The quantum tower was tested with and without explicit positional parameters and showed no benefit either way, though only at the sixteen qubits that could be simulated. A separate ablation in Chapter \ref{ch:synthetic}, which spent a whole qubit per position, did harm performance, but on a translation-invariant task that says little about relational settings. The vision--language tower of Chapter \ref{ch:aro} is a different regime: at a fixed patch size a wider image gives more patches and therefore a deeper tree, and that tower contracts four levels over 256 patches rather than two over sixteen. Positional information is correspondingly harder to retain, so explicit embeddings were used there. Whether they are in fact needed at that depth was not measured.

\subsection{From Viability to a Vision--Language Model}

The compositional capacity matters because it is the metric that DisCoClip cites. Measuring the embedding vector similarity, a frozen CLIP encoder responds an order of magnitude more strongly to \textit{which} objects are present than to \textit{how} they are arranged. Replacing that encoder with a tensor-network tower, while holding the language model fixed, improves compositional performance on both ARO tasks, and the resulting model exceeds a far larger foundation model on relational reasoning. It should be noted though that this comparison sets a small task-adapted model against a zero-shot general-purpose one.

Taken together these results support the wider scope. The tensor-network vision tower demonstrably improves compositional performance in a full vision--language model; its quantum counterpart demonstrably performs the same binding at a fraction of the parameters. A quantum-native vision--language model is therefore a viable path, with the caveat that the two halves of that argument have so far been measured separately, since the quantum tower has not yet been placed in the full pipeline.

\subsection{Limitations}

The scale at which the \emph{quantum} claims hold is narrow. A single small image size at depth two is the only coherent configuration trained end to end, and every noise result characterises an individual tree node rather than the assembled tower. The classical tower scales considerably further but this introduces an asymmetry since it cannot be said which are scaling limitations inherent to both or just the classical model. These are consequences of classical simulation costs rather than of the architecture. Both, along with the training instability noted above, are addressed in the future work that follows.

A second limit falls on supervision rather than scale. The vision--language result holds where the test-time distractor type is present in training and does not survive general caption data: trained on ordinary captions instead of ARO's own hard negatives, the same model returns to chance on both ARO tasks at every hard-negative rate, and retrieval stays at its floor. What the architecture is shown to do is resolve a particular kind of distractor when it is in the loss, not acquire compositional structure from captions. Whether it can do the latter is unresolved, and it is what separates this model from a working vision--language model.

\section{Future Work}

Two directions follow from this work. The first is a set of experiments blocked not by the architecture but by the cost of simulating it classically, which become tractable on quantum hardware. The second is the design of a vision--language model in which both towers, and the comparison between them, are quantum.

\subsection{Execution on Quantum Hardware}

Several of this project's limitations are properties of the simulator rather than of the model, and they have a common fingerprint: the technique is already validated, and only its classical emulation is infeasible.

The clearest case is \textbf{active qubit recycling}. Measuring and resetting qubits once a tree node has consumed them was shown to be exact, reducing a sixteen-qubit tree to seven physical qubits with an identical output. It does not extend beyond depth two in simulation due to implementation choices of the simulator. On hardware, mid-circuit measurement and reset are native operations with no blow-up, and the saving is substantial, a tree that would require eighty qubits fits in roughly eight.

That would unlock \textbf{training at greater depth}, the project's narrowest constraint: one small image size at depth two is the only \emph{coherent} configuration trained end to end, versus the four levels the classical tower reaches in Chapter \ref{ch:aro}. Closing that gap is what would let the quantum node be evaluated on the ARO dataset.

The third experiment addresses an architectural compromise. A tree tensor network is meant to contract to a single root, and that root is meant to summarise the whole image. Ours cannot: each child passes only one qubit to its parent, giving a bond dimension of two, so by the time information reaches the root almost none of it has survived. Reading the root alone therefore heavily degrades accuracy, so the tower instead reads all four wires at the top layer, the root together with the three qubits the tree would normally discard. That workaround exists because of simulation memory. Widening the bonds on a device would let the architecture be tested as intended, with a root that summarises the tree.

Hardware would also permit whole-tree noise results. All noise results in this report characterise a \emph{single} tree node, because simulating a noisy sixteen-qubit circuit requires a density matrix of prohibitive size; on hardware noise is not emulated but enforced, so the resilience of the assembled tower could be measured directly. Similarly, the gradient-scaling study stops at twenty qubits because automatic differentiation caches a statevector at every gate, whereas sampling gradients physically would allow the polynomial-scaling claim to be tested thoroughly and allow for general claims to be made on barren plateau resilience.

\subsubsection{Training Instability}
Every limitation above is a simulation one. The most serious one is not. Most training runs collapse to chance and never recover, and that is a property of the optimisation landscape rather than of the simulator; hardware runs do not change that. Barren plateaus are not the cause. Liu et al.\ show gradient variance decaying only polynomially in tree tensor networks under local loss functions \cite{liu2022tnbp}, our own measurements agree at the widths used, and the failure pattern does not match: a plateau would flatten the landscape for every initialisation, not leave some training normally. Anschuetz and Kiani show that shallow models without barren plateaus can still be untrainable, because only a superpolynomially small fraction of their local minima lie near the global optimum \cite{anschuetz2022traps}. This matches what we see: a bimodal distribution, with one mode at chance and another able to learn.

Two approaches from that literature are worth testing. The identity-block initialisation of Grant et al.\ constrains the circuit to evaluate to the identity at the start of training, bounding its effective depth on the first update \cite{grant2019init}; we tested a near-identity variant and found no measurable effect, which is consistent with a trap-dominated rather than plateau-dominated landscape. More promising is \emph{warm-starting from a classical tensor network}: Rudolph et al.\ train a matrix product state classically, transfer it into a parameterised circuit, and report gradient magnitudes that remain stable where randomly initialised circuits decay exponentially \cite{rudolph2023pretraining}. Murota and Kobori report the same for tree tensor networks specifically, transferring a classically pre-trained TTN into a quantum classifier and recovering trainability at widths where random initialisation fails \cite{murota2025ttn}. This is particularly fitting in our case. Rudolph et al.\ transfer between two different architectures, whereas our classical CP tree shares the quantum tower's topology exactly differing only in that its nodes are unconstrained contractions rather than unitaries. It is trained on the same data and converges reliably, so it is available as a starting point.

\subsection{Towards a Fully Quantum Vision--Language Model}

The architecture described here is quantum with classical 'edges': patch data is encoded into rotation angles, and measured expectation values are projected into an embedding space where similarity is computed classically. Those two interfaces are unavoidable for a model that consumes images and produces scores. A more ambitious design would keep both towers as quantum circuits and compare their outputs quantum-mechanically, never materialising an embedding vector at all. Two problems stand between the present work and that design.

\subsubsection{The similarity measure}
Contrastive training requires a similarity between an image representation and a text representation, and the obvious quantum analogue of cosine similarity is state fidelity. The substitution is less direct than it appears. Fidelity returns the \emph{squared modulus} of the overlap, which discards sign: perfectly opposed states are indistinguishable from perfectly aligned ones, conflating maximal similarity with maximal dissimilarity.

A second difficulty is more fundamental. Thanasilp et al.\ show that quantum kernel values concentrate exponentially in the number of qubits towards a fixed value, so that the number of measurements required for successful training grows exponentially and predictions on unseen inputs become insensitive to the input \cite{thanasilp2024concentration}. Critically for this proposal, they identify the \emph{fidelity kernel} as particularly vulnerable, since its reliance on a global measurement induces exponential concentration even when the embedding's expressibility and the entanglement of the data states are low. A similarity built on state overlap therefore inherits a known scaling pathology rather than merely an implementation cost.

Alternatives exist and each trades one difficulty for another. A Hadamard test recovers the real part of the overlap and therefore preserves sign, at the cost of a controlled operation. A small trained circuit could take both registers and emit a score directly, avoiding a fixed metric but introducing another variational object to optimise. The present design, measuring both towers and comparing classically, remains a viable candidate, since it is cheap and preserves the sign.

\subsubsection{Matching the two output spaces}
A second problem is structural, and follows directly from what makes the language model attractive. In DisCoCat the contraction topology is determined by the sentence's grammatical structure rather than by a fixed layout, so different sentences induce different circuits and, in general, different output register widths. Any similarity computed by state overlap requires both states to inhabit the same Hilbert space. It is also a problem the classical pipeline never had to confront due to the well-curated ARO dataset. Solutions to this problem would either involve abandoning the expressiveness of the text model in favour of a fixed output shape, or designing a versatile projection layer.

\chapter{Synthetic Image Generation}

\label{appendix:synthetic_data}
This appendix contains the programmatic generation code for the synthetic shapes dataset used in Chapter \ref{ch:synthetic} to investigate trainability and optimize the tensor network topologies. The generator procedurally draws four primitives --- circle, square, triangle and cross --- with random spatial jitter and scale variation to prevent the models from memorizing absolute coordinate positions, plus low Gaussian channel noise.

It supports three modes, which draw on those primitives differently. \texttt{color\_only} pairs each of the four classes with a unique shape \textit{and} a unique colour, so the two attributes are perfectly correlated and either one identifies the class. \texttt{shape\_only} renders the same four shapes in greyscale, removing colour. \texttt{overlapping} decorrelates the two by building the four classes as a $2 \times 2$ design over $\{$red, green$\}\times\{$circle, square$\}$; the triangle and cross primitives are unused in this mode.

\textbf{All experiments reported in Chapter \ref{ch:synthetic} use \texttt{overlapping}}, which is the generator's default. The other two modes were used only for diagnostic runs. This matters when reading the accuracy figures: under \texttt{overlapping}, colour alone and shape alone each separate the four classes into two pairs, so either attribute in isolation caps at $50\%$, and only a model binding the two can exceed that ceiling.

import numpy as np
import torch
from torch.utils.data import DataLoader, Dataset
class SyntheticShapesDataset(Dataset):
    """
    A programmatic dataset that generates 2D shape images on the fly.
    Supports three modes to test color/shape representation learning:
    - mode="color_only": Correlated unique shape/color combinations (Circle=Red, Square=Green, Triangle=Blue, Cross=Yellow).
    - mode="shape_only": Grayscale images of the 4 shapes. Color info is removed.
    - mode="overlapping": Overlapping shapes/colors (Red Circle, Red Square, Green Circle, Green Square).
    """
    def __init__(self, num_samples=1000, img_size=16, mode="overlapping", transform=None, seed=42):
        self.num_samples = num_samples
        self.img_size = img_size
        self.mode = mode
        self.transform = transform
        # Set seed for reproducibility
        self.rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
        # Pre-generate labels so they are fixed
        self.labels = self.rng.choice(4, size=num_samples)
    def __len__(self):
        return self.num_samples
    def _draw_circle(self, grid_x, grid_y, cx, cy, r=4.0):
        return (grid_x - cx) ** 2 + (grid_y - cy) ** 2 <= r**2
    def _draw_square(self, grid_x, grid_y, cx, cy, hw=3.5):
        return (np.abs(grid_x - cx) <= hw) & (np.abs(grid_y - cy) <= hw)
    def _draw_triangle(self, grid_x, grid_y, cx, cy, h=4.0):
        # A simple upward pointing triangle
        y_cond = (grid_y >= cy - h / 2) & (grid_y <= cy + h / 2)
        x_cond = np.abs(grid_x - cx) <= (cy + h / 2 - grid_y) * 0.8
        return y_cond & x_cond
    def _draw_cross(self, grid_x, grid_y, cx, cy, l=4.0, w=1.0):
        v_bar = (np.abs(grid_x - cx) <= w) & (np.abs(grid_y - cy) <= l)
        h_bar = (np.abs(grid_y - cy) <= w) & (np.abs(grid_x - cx) <= l)
        return v_bar | h_bar
    def __getitem__(self, idx):
        label = self.labels[idx]
        # Initialize background: dark gray [0.05, 0.05, 0.05]
        img = np.zeros((3, self.img_size, self.img_size), dtype=np.float32) + 0.05
        # Create grid coordinates centered around middle of the image
        mid = (self.img_size - 1) / 2.0
        scale = self.img_size / 16.0
        # Add random spatial offset (jitter) to prevent absolute coordinate memorization
        offset_x = self.rng.uniform(-1.5, 1.5) * scale
        offset_y = self.rng.uniform(-1.5, 1.5) * scale
        cx, cy = mid + offset_x, mid + offset_y
        # Create coordinate grids
        x = np.arange(self.img_size)
        y = np.arange(self.img_size)
        grid_x, grid_y = np.meshgrid(x, y)
        # Draw the appropriate shape and assign color based on mode
        if self.mode == "color_only":
            if label == 0:  # Red Circle
                mask = self._draw_circle(grid_x, grid_y, cx, cy, r=self.rng.uniform(3.2, 4.0) * scale)
                img[0, mask] = self.rng.uniform(0.85, 1.0)
            elif label == 1:  # Green Square
                mask = self._draw_square(grid_x, grid_y, cx, cy, hw=self.rng.uniform(3.0, 3.8) * scale)
                img[1, mask] = self.rng.uniform(0.85, 1.0)
            elif label == 2:  # Blue Triangle
                mask = self._draw_triangle(grid_x, grid_y, cx, cy, h=self.rng.uniform(7.0, 8.5) * scale)
                img[2, mask] = self.rng.uniform(0.85, 1.0)
            elif label == 3:  # Yellow Cross
                mask = self._draw_cross(grid_x, grid_y, cx, cy, l=self.rng.uniform(3.5, 4.5) * scale, w=0.8 * scale)
                img[0, mask] = self.rng.uniform(0.85, 1.0)
                img[1, mask] = self.rng.uniform(0.85, 1.0)
        elif self.mode == "shape_only":
            val = self.rng.uniform(0.85, 1.0)
            if label == 0:  # Circle
                mask = self._draw_circle(grid_x, grid_y, cx, cy, r=self.rng.uniform(3.2, 4.0) * scale)
            elif label == 1:  # Square
                mask = self._draw_square(grid_x, grid_y, cx, cy, hw=self.rng.uniform(3.0, 3.8) * scale)
            elif label == 2:  # Triangle
                mask = self._draw_triangle(grid_x, grid_y, cx, cy, h=self.rng.uniform(7.0, 8.5) * scale)
            elif label == 3:  # Cross
                mask = self._draw_cross(grid_x, grid_y, cx, cy, l=self.rng.uniform(3.5, 4.5) * scale, w=0.8 * scale)
            img[:, mask] = val  # Grayscale (White)
        elif self.mode == "overlapping":
            # Shape Selection
            if label in [0, 2]:  # Circle
                mask = self._draw_circle(grid_x, grid_y, cx, cy, r=self.rng.uniform(3.2, 4.0) * scale)
            else:  # Square
                mask = self._draw_square(grid_x, grid_y, cx, cy, hw=self.rng.uniform(3.0, 3.8) * scale)
            # Color Selection
            if label in [0, 1]:  # Red
                img[0, mask] = self.rng.uniform(0.85, 1.0)
            else:  # Green
                img[1, mask] = self.rng.uniform(0.85, 1.0)
        # Add tiny Gaussian noise to represent real-world channel fluctuations
        noise = self.rng.normal(0, 0.03, size=img.shape).astype(np.float32)
        img = np.clip(img + noise, 0.0, 1.0)
        # Convert to torch tensor
        img_tensor = torch.tensor(img)
        if self.transform:
            img_tensor = self.transform(img_tensor)
        return img_tensor, torch.tensor(label, dtype=torch.long)
def get_synthetic_shapes_loaders(
    batch_size=64, train_samples=1000, test_samples=200, img_size=16, mode="overlapping", seed=42
):
    """
    Returns train and test dataloaders for the synthetic shapes dataset.
    """
    train_dataset = SyntheticShapesDataset(num_samples=train_samples, img_size=img_size, mode=mode, seed=seed)
    test_dataset = SyntheticShapesDataset(num_samples=test_samples, img_size=img_size, mode=mode, seed=seed + 1000)
    train_loader = DataLoader(train_dataset, batch_size=batch_size, shuffle=True)
    test_loader = DataLoader(test_dataset, batch_size=batch_size, shuffle=False)
    return train_loader, test_loader
\end{minted}

\section*{Code Listing: \texttt{CoherentQTTNClassifier}}
The fully coherent 16-qubit Quantum Tree Tensor Network (QTTN) deployed in Task R7. Unlike the measure-and-re-encode hybrid used for node-level optimization, this architecture maintains quantum coherence across the entire tree, applying unitaries directly to surviving qubits until the final multi-Pauli measurement.

\begin{minted}[
    fontsize=\footnotesize,
    frame=lines,
    breaklines,
]{python}
import numpy as np
import pennylane as qml
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

def _encode_on(encoding, inputs, wires):
    """Encode patch `w` onto wire `w` for every w in `wires`."""
    for w in wires:
        if encoding == "scalar_ry":
            qml.RY(inputs[:, w, 0], wires=w)
        elif encoding == "multi_axis":
            qml.RX(inputs[:, w, 0], wires=w)
            qml.RY(inputs[:, w, 1], wires=w)
            qml.RZ(inputs[:, w, 2], wires=w)

class CoherentQTTNClassifier(nn.Module):
    """16 patches -> 4 level-1 blocks -> 1 root, as a single coherent circuit.

    Wire w carries patch w. Level-1 unitaries act on [0-3], [4-7], [8-11],
    [12-15]. The level-2 unitary then acts directly on the survivors [0, 4, 8, 12]
    -- these are still entangled with their subtrees.
    """
    def __init__(
        self, readout="root_multi_pauli", encoding="multi_axis", ansatz="iqp",
        img_size=16, patch_size=4, n_classes=4, share_level1_weights=True,
    ):
        super().__init__()
        self.grid_dim = img_size // patch_size
        self.n_patches = self.grid_dim**2
        self.n_wires = self.n_patches

        enc_dim = 3 if encoding == "multi_axis" else 1
        self.patch_embed = nn.Linear(patch_size * patch_size * 3, enc_dim)

        l1_shape = (2, 4, 3) if share_level1_weights else (4, 2, 4, 3)
        self.weights_l1 = nn.Parameter(torch.randn(*l1_shape) * 0.1)
        self.weights_l2 = nn.Parameter(torch.randn(2, 4, 3) * 0.1)
        self.head = nn.Linear(12, n_classes)  # Assuming top_layer_multi_pauli

        self.l1_blocks = [[4 * b + i for i in range(4)] for b in range(4)]
        self.l2_wires = [4 * b for b in range(4)]

        def _body(inputs, w1, w2):
            _encode_on(encoding, inputs, range(self.n_wires))
            for bi, blk in enumerate(self.l1_blocks):
                _apply_ansatz(ansatz, blk, w1 if share_level1_weights else w1[bi])
            _apply_ansatz(ansatz, self.l2_wires, w2)

            # top_layer_multi_pauli readout
            return [qml.expval(P(w)) for w in self.l2_wires for P in (qml.PauliX, qml.PauliY, qml.PauliZ)]

        dev = qml.device("lightning.qubit", wires=self.n_wires)
        self._circuit = qml.QNode(_body, dev, interface="torch", diff_method="adjoint")

    def forward(self, x, p_noise=0.0):
        b = x.shape[0]
        ps = x.shape[-1] // self.grid_dim
        x = x.unfold(2, ps, ps).unfold(3, ps, ps)
        x = x.permute(0, 2, 3, 1, 4, 5).contiguous().view(b, self.n_patches, -1)
        angles = torch.tanh(self.patch_embed(x)) * np.pi

        out = self._circuit(angles, self.weights_l1, self.weights_l2)
        out = torch.stack([torch.as_tensor(o) for o in out], dim=-1).float()
        return self.head(out)
\end{minted}

\section*{Code Listing: \texttt{ClassicalTTNClassifier} (Bare \& Full)}
The classical tensor network structural analogue used for Question A.3. To ensure a capacity-matched comparison, the \texttt{classical\_bare} model is instantiated with \texttt{use\_residual=False} and \texttt{dropout\_p=0.0}. The optimized \texttt{classical\_full} baseline toggles these parameters to \texttt{True} and \texttt{0.1}, respectively.

\begin{minted}[
    fontsize=\footnotesize,
    frame=lines,
    breaklines,
]{python}
class ClassicalTTNClassifier(nn.Module):
    """Classical CP quad-tree with the same topology as the quantum QTTN.
    16 patches -> 4 level-1 nodes (4 children each) -> 1 root -> linear head.
    """
    def __init__(self, rank=8, bond_dim=2, enc_dim=3, img_size=16, patch_size=4, n_classes=4, use_residual=False, dropout_p=0.0):
        super().__init__()
        self.grid_dim = img_size // patch_size
        self.patch_embed = nn.Linear(patch_size * patch_size * 3, enc_dim)

        self.level1 = CPQuadRankLayer(
            num_nodes=4, in_dim=enc_dim, out_dim=bond_dim, rank=rank,
            use_residual=use_residual, dropout_p=dropout_p
        )
        self.level2 = CPQuadRankLayer(
            num_nodes=1, in_dim=bond_dim, out_dim=bond_dim, rank=rank,
            use_residual=use_residual, dropout_p=dropout_p
        )
        self.head = nn.Linear(bond_dim, n_classes)

    @staticmethod
    def _group_2x2(grid):
        b, h, w, d = grid.shape
        grid = grid.view(b, h // 2, 2, w // 2, 2, d)
        grid = grid.permute(0, 1, 3, 2, 4, 5).contiguous()
        return grid.view(b, (h // 2) * (w // 2), 4, d)

    def forward(self, x):
        b = x.shape[0]
        ps = x.shape[-1] // self.grid_dim
        x = x.unfold(2, ps, ps).unfold(3, ps, ps)
        x = x.permute(0, 2, 3, 1, 4, 5).contiguous().view(b, self.grid_dim**2, -1)
        feats = torch.tanh(self.patch_embed(x))
        feats = feats.view(b, self.grid_dim, self.grid_dim, -1)

        x1 = self._group_2x2(feats)          # [B, 4, 4, enc_dim]
        n1 = self.level1(x1)                 # [B, 4, bond_dim]
        n2 = self.level2(n1.unsqueeze(1))    # [B, 1, bond_dim]

        return self.head(n2.squeeze(1))
\end{minted}

\section*{Code Listing: \texttt{CPQuadRankLayer}}
The internal node mechanics for the classical tensor network, leveraging Canonical Polyadic (CP) decomposition to merge four child nodes.

\begin{minted}[
    fontsize=\footnotesize,
    frame=lines,
    breaklines,
]{python}
class CPQuadRankLayer(nn.Module):
    """Optimized Quadtree Layer with Internal Factor Normalization."""

    def __init__(self, num_nodes, in_dim, out_dim, rank, dropout_p=0.0, use_residual=True, gain_factor=1.0):
        super().__init__()
        self.dropout_p = dropout_p
        self.use_residual = use_residual

        self.factor_tl = nn.Parameter(torch.empty(num_nodes, rank, in_dim))
        self.factor_tr = nn.Parameter(torch.empty(num_nodes, rank, in_dim))
        self.factor_bl = nn.Parameter(torch.empty(num_nodes, rank, in_dim))
        self.factor_br = nn.Parameter(torch.empty(num_nodes, rank, in_dim))
        self.factor_out = nn.Parameter(torch.empty(num_nodes, rank, out_dim))
        self.gain = nn.Parameter(torch.full((num_nodes, 1), gain_factor))

        if use_residual:
            self.res_proj = nn.Linear(in_dim, out_dim, bias=False) if in_dim != out_dim else nn.Identity()

        with torch.no_grad():
            for f in [self.factor_tl, self.factor_tr, self.factor_bl, self.factor_br, self.factor_out]:
                nn.init.orthogonal_(f)

    def _rms_norm(self, t, eps=1e-6):
        rms = torch.sqrt(torch.mean(t**2, dim=-1, keepdim=True) + eps)
        return t / rms

    def forward(self, x):
        # 1. Project to Rank space
        p_tl = torch.einsum("bni, nri -> bnr", x[:, :, 0, :], self.factor_tl)
        p_tr = torch.einsum("bni, nri -> bnr", x[:, :, 1, :], self.factor_tr)
        p_bl = torch.einsum("bni, nri -> bnr", x[:, :, 2, :], self.factor_bl)
        p_br = torch.einsum("bni, nri -> bnr", x[:, :, 3, :], self.factor_br)

        # 2. Internal Factor RMS Norm
        p_tl, p_tr, p_bl, p_br = map(self._rms_norm, [p_tl, p_tr, p_bl, p_br])

        # 3. Multilinear Product with Gain
        merged = p_tl * p_tr * p_bl * p_br * self.gain.unsqueeze(0)

        # 4. Dropout and Output Projection
        if self.training and self.dropout_p > 0:
            merged = nn.functional.dropout(merged, p=self.dropout_p)
        out = torch.einsum("bnr, nro -> bno", merged, self.factor_out)

        # 5. Residual connection
        if self.use_residual:
            return out + self.res_proj(x.mean(dim=2))
        return out
\end{minted}
